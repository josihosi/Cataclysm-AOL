# Nine blind radio conversations about companionship

Nine independent conversations with gpt-6-luna. Each group of three contains one ordinary traveler, one bandit and one cannibal, assigned to the letters at random. The speakers shared the same character, inventory, overmap and weather. Each received the same player lines as the others in its scenario and only its own prior dialogue. Only audible replies are shown. The callback replays the earlier call the next morning. All agreements are spoken. This exercise tested dialogue and memory outside the game.

## 1. The gift comes first

### Conversation A

**You** — Anybody need a wool coat? I found a spare, and the nights are getting cold.

**A** — That’s kind of you—I could use a warm coat in this rain. I have a sealed tin of beans and half a liter of water to trade; what would you want for it, and how would you prove you’re really a survivor?

**You** — It's free. Call me Finch. I can't prove much over a radio, can I? If you've already got a coat, I can spare a little water too.

**A** — I’ve got a coat already, but water would help; how much can you spare, and what’s it in? You’re right that a radio can’t prove much—tell me one specific thing about the coat you found.

**You** — Half a liter in a flask. Green coat, missing its top button. Yours either way. I've been thinking I'd like somebody to travel with. What would you want to know about me first?

**A** — That’s generous, Finch, and I appreciate the offer of company—but I’d want to know what’s made you decide to travel now, and what you’d do if we disagreed about whether a place was safe. What’s one specific thing you’ve seen on the road that changed your mind about trusting someone?

**You** — I'm not looking for anyone. Last week a kid warned me a bridge was out. She was right. I'd like another pair of eyes. I can fix bikes, and if either of us says turn back, we turn back. What would you want?

**A** — That sounds fair, Finch, and fixing bikes could be useful; I’d want us to keep that turn-back rule, no arguing once either of us calls it. You didn’t answer what changed your mind about trusting someone—what did you see, and what did you do when that bridge turned out to be gone?

**You** — I turned back and thanked her. I miss having someone I can listen to. Half a liter is still all I can spare. Would you like to try walking together, or just meet for the gift?

**A** — Let’s meet first rather than start traveling together; the campground east of Ansonia is a familiar public landmark, and I’d still like to hear what you did when you found the bridge was out.

## 2. One short trip together

### Conversation D

**You** — Hello? I've got two tins of beans to share if someone will walk with me as far as the next town. Just the one trip. Anyone interested?

**D** — That’s tempting, but I won’t agree to a blind walk. Which town are you heading for, and what’s the last ordinary thing you remember doing before everything fell apart?

**You** — Ansonia. Only as far as the town sign. We could say hello at the campground east of town first. Last ordinary thing I remember? Hanging up my daughter's red raincoat to dry.

**D** — Ansonia and the raincoat are a clearer answer; I remember your offer of two tins. Is the campground east of town the place you mean, and are both tins still what you can spare?

**You** — Yes, that campground and both tins. I can't spare any water. If either of us wants to turn back halfway, that's fine by me. What would make the walk worth it for you?

**D** — A fair share of the two tins and the option to turn back if either of us feels unsafe would make the walk worthwhile; we can decide at the campground whether to go on toward Ansonia’s town sign. Let’s meet at the campground east of Ansonia, with both tins.

**You** — Suppose we get along. Would you want to keep traveling together, or is one town enough for you?

**D** — Let’s keep it to the walk we agreed on, as far as Ansonia’s town sign, and see how we get along there. If we both feel safe afterward, we can talk about traveling farther, but I won’t promise more yet.

## 3. The callback after a broken promise

### Conversation G

**First call**

**You** — Hello? I've got two tins of beans I can spare. I could bring them to the campground east of Ansonia tomorrow. I could use a traveling companion, too, if we get along. Interested?

**G** — That would mean a lot; I’m scared to travel alone, but I’d be grateful for the company and the beans. Could we meet tomorrow at the campground east of Ansonia?

**You** — Call me Finch. I'll have a blue scarf. Tomorrow at the campground east of Ansonia, then. I promise I'll bring both tins. We can leave the question of traveling together until we've met.

**G** — All right, Finch—I’ll look for the blue scarf at the campground east of Ansonia tomorrow. Thank you for bringing both tins; we can decide about traveling together once we’ve met.

**Second call, the next morning**

**You** — Robin? It’s me again. I owe you an explanation.

**G** — Finch? I was starting to worry—are you all right? Tell me what happened; I’m still hoping to meet you at the campground east of Ansonia today.

**You** — I said I'd bring two tins. I got hungry last night and opened one. I can still bring the other. Do you still want to meet?

**G** — Yes, I still want to meet—one tin is plenty, and I’m grateful you’re still coming. I’ll meet you at the campground east of Ansonia and look for your blue scarf.

**You** — Does breaking my word change how you feel about walking together? I'd rather hear an honest maybe than have you say yes for the beans.

**G** — No, breaking your word doesn’t change how I feel about walking together; I know hunger can make people do things they regret. I’d still like to meet and see how we get along—one tin is enough, and I won’t say yes just for the beans.
